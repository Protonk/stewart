This is a request to challenge two general commitments the user and I reached
while deciding what Stewart's close must account for before it discards
material. I am not asking you to design mechanisms, grant authority or judge
implementation facts. No attachments are required; the context you need is
below.

Background. Stewart's close is the only deletion in the system. It removes the
auditor's checkout, the supplied runtime, any runtime recovery copies,
experiment HOME directories, and the private session that holds the captured
collections and receipts. Retention and export, which the manager runs before
close, decide what survives. The user has decided that retention will not be
made mandatory before close: that would be invasive to the manager's flow.

We settled the close question by reframing it. Close offers the manager an
opportunity to keep material, not preservation. Each removed surface must
match a capture the manager could have reviewed, be verifiably regenerable
from retained records, or be discarded by name in the closing acknowledgment,
whose record freezes the identity (paths, sizes, digests) of what was
discarded. This extends an existing rule: Stewart acts only on what the
closing evaluation judged.

Along the way we reached two general commitments that will shape later work,
and I would like them attacked.

Commitment 1: Stewart's refusals are limited to its own claims and safety.
The test for any refusal is: what claim would Stewart make falsely by
proceeding? If none, Stewart does not refuse. The legitimate refusals we
identified are: declining to vouch for bytes it cannot verify, while still
saying where they are; declining to follow a planted link or read a special
file; declining to destroy an exclusive destination, another audit's live use,
or material the closing evaluation did not judge; and conditioning its own
claims, such as requiring the manager's disposition of gaps before publishing
a report under Stewart's custody claims. A corollary: a refusal whose next
step cannot be performed inside Stewart is suspect, because it pushes the
manager outside custody to resolve it. The motivating case: today a verified
report cannot be exported if a sibling capture in the same collection was
damaged later, even though the report's own hash chain verifies. We decided
it can be, carrying the damage explicitly and preserving the damaged original.

Commitment 2: Stewart makes no material-preservation promise to anyone over
the deciding manager. We enumerated who could be owed a promise to preserve
material regardless of what the manager says at close: the manager's own
earlier judgment against its present command (existing: a resource claim is
released rather than acted on when the resource changed); the manager role
across sessions (served by records, not material); other audits with a live
use (an existing material promise); later readers who were party to nothing
(served by records); the human principal behind the manager (no material
promise; mandatory retention declined); the auditor (process promises only:
raw capture before interpretation, a disposition of every gap, attribution
never turned into a verdict); and Stewart's own mechanisms (invariants that
serve the record readers). Conclusion: Stewart makes outcome promises about
records, and about material only toward other live uses and the manager's
frozen judgment. The product is meant eventually to accommodate a human
auditor.

What I am asking:

1. Scenarios in which withholding a verified report from the manager is
   nevertheless right, under the Commitment 1 test or against it. If you find
   one, say what false claim proceeding would make, or why the test is the
   wrong test.
2. Parties missing from the Commitment 2 enumeration, or a listed party whose
   treatment is wrong. The human-auditor case is where I am least sure: is
   "the manager commissioned the work and owns the deliverable, and the
   auditor holds their own copy" sufficient, or does a human auditor change
   what Stewart owes?
3. Whether "opportunity, not preservation" has a hole: a case where the
   manager had the opportunity in form only and the loss is one Stewart should
   have prevented.

Please treat the account above as my understanding, not as established fact
about Stewart. Repository-specific claims will still need evidence here.
