# Riker advice on experiment reporting

Relayed by the principal on 2026-11-07 in response to the [reporting request](APPARATUS-REPORTING-REQUEST.md) and [design brief](APPARATUS-REPORTING-BRIEF.md). The reply follows verbatim. It is consultation evidence, not an implementation assignment or spending authorization.

I agree with the question-first assembled report as our working preference, but the important commitment is to **one coherent ordinary reading, not necessarily one artifact or one kind of authority**. Checked observations and the experimenter's attributed interpretation should remain distinguishable even when presented together.

Three properties matter most:

1. **No authority laundering.** Checked observations, authored interpretations, permissions and decisions retain their respective sources and authority. Assembly must not turn an interpretation into an apparatus-certified finding.
2. **No reconstruction burden.** The principal should understand what the experiment established, what it did not establish, what was spent, whether the block is accounted for, and what remains to be done without reconstructing the history. Supporting records remain inspectable.
3. **No false finality.** A completed experimental block need not settle its scientific question. Missing evidence, unexercised mechanisms, unresolved judgments and deferred billing must remain visible without preventing legitimate completion of other work.

**Keep the apparatus's understanding of intellectual work deliberately small.** It needs to know what was commissioned and authorized, what operations occurred, what evidence exists, what its checks establish, and the state of custody and resources. It may perform narrow, explicitly defined study-specific checks when the evidence supports them. It should not acquire a general theory of answer quality, successful reasoning, satisfactory investigation or model competence. For an auditor run that is not grossly out of whack, Stewart should care very little about intellectual work broadly construed. The experimenter can evaluate that work, attribute the assessment and connect it to evidence. Neither Stewart nor its apparatus should silently inherit that judgment.

The examples illustrate distinctions worth preserving in ordinary reporting. An expected turn-limit stop is not necessarily an operational failure; manager closure is not auditor submission; a successful answer is not evidence that an unoffered affordance was exercised. An absent observation in complete evidence differs from an observation made unknowable by missing evidence. Later custody records can establish completion without making an earlier, correctly dated report false. Estimates, calculated costs and independent billing observations are different things.

I also favor allowing a checked technical return to exist before the experimenter's interpretation is ready. Intellectual assessment should not gate custody or evidence handling. The eventual study result should nevertheless give the principal a coherent account of what the observations mean for the agreed question.

Finally, don't optimize the apparatus toward producing unambiguous scientific verdicts. Optimize for **an intelligible and well-supported account of the evidentiary position**, including surprising, negative and inconclusive findings. The experiment should remain free to discover something other than what its designers anticipated.

These are design commitments, not a prescribed architecture or implementation sequence. The future autonomy and paid-launch agreement remain separate.
