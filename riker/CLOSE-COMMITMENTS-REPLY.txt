My assessment is that Commitment 1 has a genuine defect in its refusal test; Commitment 2 is defensible as a default but too absolute; and “opportunity, not preservation” is sound only if opportunity means something stronger than formal availability.

I would retain the decision to export an independently verified report despite damage elsewhere in its collection. The counterexamples below do not justify returning to collection-wide refusal.

## 1. When withholding a verified report is right

### A. Verification does not establish a right to disclose

Suppose the auditor's verified report contains the identity of a confidential informant, or material obtained outside the authorized inquiry. The manager commissioned the audit but is not authorized to receive that particular information.

Stewart can establish that the report's bytes are exactly those submitted. It cannot thereby establish that giving those bytes to this manager is permissible.

The transfer can be wrong without Stewart making any false claim.

This defeats the proposed test:

> What claim would Stewart make falsely by proceeding?

One could rescue the test by saying every action implicitly asserts that the action is authorized. But then almost any wrongful operation becomes a false-claim case, and the test loses its ability to distinguish legitimate refusals from invented ones.

The distinction is between what Stewart may truthfully say and what Stewart may legitimately do. They overlap, but neither subsumes the other.

This counterexample is conditional. If the commission genuinely authorizes the manager to receive everything in the report, it disappears. My objection is to treating commissioning as sufficient authorization in every case.

### B. The report is verified, but the offered custody claim is false

This is a case your existing test handles correctly.

Suppose the report's bytes verify, but a sibling capture has been damaged. Exporting the report through a presentation that represents the entire collection as complete would make a false claim.

The correct refusal concerns that presentation, not necessarily the report.

If Stewart can deliver the report while truthfully identifying the sibling damage, it should. It need not endorse the auditor's conclusions or assert that every item of supporting evidence survives.

This supports your motivating decision, with an important distinction: verification of an individual report, verification of its attribution, and completeness of the evidentiary collection are separate claims.

### C. Performing the export would itself cause an impermissible effect

Suppose delivering the verified report requires overwriting a destination that belongs to another party. The report is sound; the destination is not available for that use.

There need be no false representation. The objection concerns the action's effects.

You already recognize this kind of refusal under safety. The point is that it makes the false-claim test insufficient even within your own enumeration.

The same observation applies to the corollary about having a next step inside Stewart.

If export requires authorization from an external rights-holder, Stewart may have no internal operation that resolves the refusal. That is not evidence that the refusal is illegitimate. Some legitimate boundaries cannot be discharged by the system enforcing them.

My judgment on Commitment 1: Keep the strong presumption against withholding a sound report. Replace the universal false-claim test with a narrower principle: Stewart should not withhold material merely to protect the appearance of completeness, but it must still respect the limits of legitimate action and outstanding commitments.

An incorrect auditor conclusion, unresolved premise, or damaged sibling ordinarily warrants qualification, not withholding.

## 2. Missing parties and the human auditor

I find three significant omissions or misclassifications.

### A. The owner or provider of the material

The manager may have commissioned the audit without owning every resource brought into Stewart.

For example, a third party supplies a runtime for inspection on the understanding that it will remain available for another purpose. The manager then orders close, which would destroy that runtime.

There may be a material obligation to the provider even if no other audit is currently using it.

This exposes a limitation in the category other audits with a live use. Other audits are one kind of claimant, not the whole category.

The relevant distinction is whether the manager has the right to dispose of the particular material, including any limitations arising from how Stewart received it.

Not every provider is owed preservation. But a provider to whom preservation or return was promised is a straightforward counterexample to Commitment 2 as written.

### B. Parties relying on an earlier material commitment

Imagine audit A has completed. Its retained outcome makes an explicit commitment that certain supporting evidence remains available for inspection. Audit B later uses the same material.

Closing B would destroy the last surviving copy of evidence required by A's commitment.

No other audit has a live use in the sense of an active investigation. Nevertheless, the prior commitment remains outstanding.

A record documenting that B destroyed those bytes does not fulfill A's earlier promise of access to them.

The category missing here is not another role but an unfulfilled material obligation. Such an obligation can survive the activity that created it.

This also exposes a weakness in saying that later readers are served by records. That is correct when they were promised only an accurate history. It is insufficient when somebody has already promised them inspectable material.

A digest and a precise record of destruction establish what was lost. They cannot substitute for the lost bytes when inspection was itself part of the promise.

### C. The human principal may not have delegated destruction

Your treatment of the human principal is sound only under an authority assumption: the deciding manager is entitled to choose loss on the principal's behalf.

An agent can be empowered to commission an investigation and receive its report without being empowered to destroy every surviving copy of its evidence.

This does not require mandatory retention for all managers. It means the manager's role does not, by itself, establish the full extent of their authority.

In an agent-operated system, this distinction matters particularly because the party issuing the close command may be several delegations removed from the party that owns the material.

The opposite case also matters: when the manager really does possess the relevant authority and no other commitment exists, an absent principal should not become an imagined veto merely because the loss is irreversible.

### D. Does a human auditor change the obligations?

I would distinguish two cases.

Case one: the auditor genuinely possesses an independent copy.

The human auditor has a lawful, complete copy of their report and the material needed to support their own professional claims. Stewart records enough provenance to connect that copy with what was submitted. No continuing access or retention was promised.

In that case, I see no general reason to give the auditor an independent right to prevent the manager from closing Stewart's copies.

Human authorship alone does not create a preservation veto.

Case two: Stewart holds the auditor's only usable copy.

Consider a human auditor working in a restricted environment where they cannot lawfully make external copies. Their working notes, observations, and unfinished report reside only in the experiment HOME that close will destroy.

The auditor has relied on Stewart as the place where their work exists. The manager may own the commissioned deliverable, but that does not necessarily settle the auditor's interest in their working materials, their ability to substantiate attributed findings, or any independently imposed professional retention obligation.

If Stewart has undertaken to provide a usable workspace or safeguard submitted work until handoff, immediate destruction could defeat a promise to the auditor.

This is stronger when the auditor is still working or has not had the agreed opportunity to hand over their material.

A human auditor therefore changes the analysis not through an automatic entitlement to indefinite retention, but through the possibility of independent reliance, ownership, confidentiality, and professional obligations.

The statement “the manager owns the deliverable, and the auditor holds their own copy” can be sufficient. But both halves must actually be true, and commissioning alone does not prove the ownership half.

### E. One listed party may not be a separate claimant

I would reconsider treating the manager's earlier judgment as a party owed material preservation.

Your example—releasing a resource claim when the resource has changed—seems fundamentally about the identity and scope of an authorized action. The earlier decision concerned particular material; it does not authorize an operation upon different material.

That is not quite a preservation promise to the manager's former self.

An earlier manager can certainly create an obligation that survives a later instruction. But its binding force would come from the obligation undertaken, not simply from being earlier.

This distinction helps avoid turning the frozen judgment into a general inability to revise decisions.

## 3. Where “opportunity, not preservation” can fail

I think the decisive question is:

If the manager had chosen to keep the material, was there a genuine possibility that Stewart would have preserved it?

If the answer is no, the opportunity was only nominal.

### A. Material could be reviewed but could not be kept

Suppose Stewart captured a report correctly. The manager can inspect it, but a separate damaged item makes the only available retention/export operation fail.

At close, the manager is shown the report's identity and can acknowledge its destruction.

Formally, the material was reviewable. In substance, the manager never had a working preservation choice.

This is exactly the underlying problem that motivated your sibling-damage decision, but it generalizes beyond collections.

The system should not represent an impossible preservation option as an opportunity that the manager declined.

The manager might nevertheless knowingly choose destruction after discovering that preservation is unavailable. That would be an explicit acceptance of loss, not evidence that the original opportunity was genuine.

### B. The closing acknowledgment records a loss the manager could not evaluate

Consider material existing only inside an experiment HOME. Stewart presents its path, size, and digest in the closing acknowledgment, but the manager has never been able to inspect its contents or understand its relationship to the auditor's findings.

The loss is identified precisely. Yet the manager's decision may have been uninformed in a way for which Stewart is responsible.

There is an important boundary here.

Stewart cannot guarantee that a manager reads everything, grasps its significance, or chooses wisely. Nor should it demand those things.

But there is a difference between a manager declining an available examination and Stewart never making examination possible.

A closing acknowledgment is excellent evidence of what was destroyed. It is not, by itself, evidence that the manager had a meaningful opportunity to decide whether those bytes should survive.

The manager can explicitly waive preservation of unexamined material. Such a waiver is different from claiming that the material was reviewable.

### C. Regeneration is possible in theory, not in reality

Suppose Stewart regards an experiment's output as regenerable because it has retained the inputs and a recipe.

But regeneration also depends on a temporary credential, remote service state, nondeterministic observation, or environmental detail that close will destroy.

The retained records may identify the intended result and contain its digest without containing enough information to reconstruct it.

If close proceeds on the premise of verifiable regenerability, the material disappears under a false premise.

This case is especially important because successful digest verification of a regenerated object would prove equality, but possession of the expected digest alone does not prove that regeneration is possible.

The distinction is between knowing what bytes to expect and being capable of producing them.

### D. The manager had already chosen preservation

A more decisive counterexample avoids disputes over how informed the manager was.

Suppose the manager properly chose to retain an item. Stewart accepted that decision, but the export failed. Close then destroys the original and describes it as discarded material.

The closing acknowledgment may accurately identify the loss. It cannot convert a failed preservation undertaking into a decision to discard.

The loss here is one Stewart should have prevented, assuming the undertaking remained binding and the manager had not subsequently withdrawn it.

This is fully compatible with refusing to make retention mandatory. Optional commitments become obligations once undertaken. The manager need not choose preservation, but Stewart cannot disregard that choice after accepting it.

## Where I would leave the commitments

I would make three conceptual adjustments.

First, treat truthful claims and legitimate actions as separate constraints. Refusal is justified by a specific false custody claim, an impermissible act, or an outstanding commitment—not by discomfort with incomplete evidence. If a verified report can be delivered truthfully and legitimately, sibling damage is no reason to withhold it.

Second, replace the hierarchy of people with a distinction between default control and independent obligations. The authorized manager ordinarily determines what survives. But the manager cannot extinguish an obligation to another party merely because Stewart is closing. Human auditors, principals, material providers, and downstream readers do not automatically gain retention rights; any rights they do possess depend on the promises, authority, and reliance actually involved.

Third, distinguish three facts that your present close formulation risks treating as interchangeable: material was available to preserve, material was deliberately surrendered, and material was lost despite an intention to preserve it. All three can be documented honestly, but they are not equivalent outcomes.

I do not think these objections defeat the governing idea of opportunity rather than mandatory preservation. They establish its limits.

The strongest version of your position, as I see it, is:

> Stewart need not save material merely because it is valuable or may matter later. It must honor material obligations it has actually undertaken, must not treat an unavailable preservation choice as one the manager declined, and must not use an accurate record of destruction to excuse a loss that violated an existing commitment.

That leaves room for irreversible, deliberate, properly authorized loss. It also preserves the distinction between custody of a truthful history and a promise that the underlying evidence will remain recoverable.

The main challenge I would carry forward is not whether another party deserves preservation over the manager. It is whether Stewart has already incurred an obligation concerning particular material that the present manager's close decision is insufficient to discharge. That question cuts across all the roles in your enumeration without making retention universally mandatory.
