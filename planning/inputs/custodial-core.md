# Proposal: A Narrow Custodial Core and a Files-Only Audit Mode

## 1. Design decisions that could simplify Stewart

These are **separable architectural decisions**, not a single package of implementation choices. Stewart may accept the narrow custodial remit without immediately implementing `--files-only`; it may adopt filesystem-only workspaces without adopting every proposed capture technique. This section describes the responsibilities and promises, not the mechanisms.

**A. Make three responsibilities exhaustive.** Stewart's privileged operational remit would consist of exactly three things:

1. **Workspace access:** establish, populate, constrain, and revoke a bounded workspace for a commissioned inquiry. Preparing the directory and arranging the technical conditions under which the auditor may use it are Stewart's job. They are not prerequisites the user must perform manually.
2. **Historical inspection:** when authorized, provide bounded, read-only observations of Git material through a controlled interface. This facility may be withheld entirely for a particular commission.
3. **Custody capture:** preserve and make recoverable the commission, what Stewart supplied or observed, authorized snapshots and submitted evidence, the auditor's return, exceptions and failures, and the manager's subsequent disposition.

These are three kinds of privileged effect, **not** a demand for exactly three commands, processes, or modules. They are also not the entirety of the custody record. Commission, permission, action, observation, return, completion, capture status, and disposition remain distinct facts within it. Recording them does not create a fourth service.

The division between people and software stays firm. The **manager** authorizes the question, scope, conditions, and handling of the outcome. The **auditor** investigates and reports. **Stewart** grants the authorized facilities and preserves their circumstances and products. It does not choose tests, plan an investigation, assess the truth of a report, apply proposed patches, automatically commission follow-up work, or decide closure. An executor may run the auditor, but execution logistics must not smuggle investigative or managerial authority into Stewart. Stewart can directly arrange or invoke the setup needed for its own workspace and custody guarantees.

**B. Add `--files-only` as a restricted commission profile.** Stewart would give the auditor an ordinary directory materialized from the authorized revision, with no `.git` supplied, no access to the source or custody repositories, and no historical-inspection facility. Stewart would configure the managed environment so Git tooling is unavailable where that restriction can be enforced. The auditor works with files and permitted non-Git tools; it is never required to stage, commit, stash, or package evidence. Stewart takes responsibility for translating between the pinned source and the directory, and between the surviving directory and durable custody.

This is not an alternative custodial system. It exercises **workspace access** and **custody capture**, while withholding **historical inspection**. A standard audit may authorize the historical interface without giving the auditor write authority over Git state. `--files-only` should be recorded in the commission, rather than treated as a transient CLI preference. Its name avoids Git's existing, unrelated meaning of *shallow*.

**C. Define custody narrowly enough to be true.** A commission records the pinned subject, question, intended decision, authorized scope, stop condition, permitted facilities, capture policy, and retention promise. Stewart must later be able to recover the commission, the auditor's submitted return and designated evidence, its own observations and custody receipts, and accurately described failures or omissions. The auditor's return kind (`answer`, `needs-evidence`, or `reframe`) is separate from whether the work finished; both are separate from whether capture succeeded and what the manager decided.

A directory capture preserves **surviving contents at a supported boundary**, not every state the auditor passed through. Capture copies evidence; it does not itself reset or destroy the directory. Initially, `--files-only` would capture after the supervised auditor session has ended and no writer can still alter the workspace. Mid-session capture is an optional capability only if whole-session quiescence can actually be guaranteed.

**D. Keep custody authority separate from subject authority.** The auditor must not be able to modify Stewart's source or custody state, and capturing an audit should not change the subject repository's refs. This is an authority boundary, not a suspicion that every auditor is malicious. Ordinary tools, accidental edits, and unusual directory contents are sufficient reasons for it.

**The admission rule:** Every privileged Stewart operation must (i) be owned by exactly one of the three responsibilities, (ii) have authority traceable to the commission or subsequent manager authorization, (iii) yield an intelligible record of its result or failure, and (iv) avoid substituting Stewart's judgment for the manager's or auditor's. A feature that fails this test belongs in an external manager/auditor/executor facility, if it belongs anywhere. This rule is the means of *insisting* on the trio rather than merely naming it.

## 2. Git cleverness—and other machinery—that could enact those decisions

Git's useful peculiarity is that **stored objects, the index, the working tree, and named refs are separable**. Stewart can use Git's content-addressed storage without exposing a Git repository to the auditor, requiring a Git index in the auditor's directory, or changing the subject branch. The original `git stash create` idea demonstrates that uncommitted file states can become durable Git objects without a conventional commit workflow. We should keep that insight, not necessarily that command.

**Materialize a workspace without checking out a repository.** Stewart can read the authorized tree with `git ls-tree` and stream blob bytes with `git cat-file --batch` or equivalent object access. A Stewart-controlled writer can create the corresponding files, directories, executable modes, and symlinks in a new ordinary directory, then make the completed directory available to the auditor. There is no `.git` to leave behind and no need for the auditor to set up anything. Workspace creation, ownership, sandbox configuration, and access revocation are ordinary filesystem and execution-environment work; Git supplies the source objects, not the isolation mechanism.

**Lift a directory into Git without turning it into a Git worktree.** Once the workspace is quiescent, a constrained scanner can enumerate its actual contents according to the commission's inclusion rules. For each supported file it can stream the exact bytes to a protected object store with `git hash-object -w --no-filters --stdin` (or equivalent raw blob creation) and build a manifest of paths, modes, and object IDs. The custody side can then construct the tree using a **private temporary index**, not the auditor's directory:

```
validated directory manifest
    → raw Git blob objects
    → GIT_INDEX_FILE + git update-index --index-info
    → git write-tree
    → git commit-tree
    → git update-ref in Stewart's custody repository
```

`git write-tree` creates a content-addressed snapshot; `git commit-tree` can give that tree a durable commit identity without moving the auditor's branch or working files; `git update-ref` makes it reachable for retention. A parentless custody commit can keep the source revision and policy in a separate receipt instead of pretending that the audit result is an ordinary descendant of the subject. The custody operation need not set `GIT_WORK_TREE` or let Git walk the auditor's directory at all. A separate durable log or artifact store may hold information that Git trees cannot represent.

**Use identity and comparison without claiming more than they prove.** The source revision and its tree provide a baseline. `git diff-tree` can describe captured additions, modifications, deletions, and mode changes; a repeated identical Git tree has the same object ID in the same object format. These are useful checks for scope violations, fail-fast conditions, and repeat captures. They attest to what was imported, not to the completeness of the scanning operation. The snapshot manifest and capture receipt remain essential.

**Offer history as a controlled observation, not a repository grant.** In the standard mode, Stewart can expose typed operations equivalent to selected `show`, `diff`, `log`, `blame`, or object-inspection queries against a declared, pinned history view. It can record the question, effective view, bounded result, and denial or failure. The auditor does not need a general privileged Git shell. Alternate historical views, including replacement refs, could be supported when explicitly commissioned; their configuration becomes part of the evidence, not an invisible property of the machine.

**Maintain a separate custody store.** Stewart can protect synthetic snapshot commits and refs in its own repository rather than inserting `refs/audit/*` into the subject repository. Object sharing or alternates may be optimizations if carefully managed; they are not necessary to the design. Equally, Git need not be the only persistence tool: receipts, exceptional filesystem objects, lifecycle events, and process-supervision attestations may live in a complementary durable store.

The cleverness is therefore broader than `stash create`: **Stewart can lend out a plain folder, take it back as content-addressed evidence, and keep the auditor entirely out of its Git bookkeeping.** Filesystem APIs, sandbox controls, process supervision, and durable recordkeeping do indispensable work alongside Git. None of these mechanisms needs to be deployed adversarially to be useful.

## 3. Traps that could sink implementation

**A. A live directory is not a snapshot.** “Freeze the workspace” is not an implementation plan. On macOS, Stewart should not assume it can freeze an arbitrary process tree or take an unprivileged, directory-scoped APFS snapshot. An agent can return from a tool call while background processes still write, or a detached process may survive the apparent end of its session. The initial capture trigger should therefore be a supervised session exit **plus a demonstrated exclusion of all remaining writers**, including outside actors with access to the directory. A blocked model turn or a single stopped PID is insufficient. If Stewart cannot prove quiescence, it cannot claim a consistent complete capture. Earlier deleted states, process memory, and unsaved work are not recoverable merely because a later directory was preserved.

**B. Materialization must reproduce stored bytes, not Git's interpretation of a checkout.** `git checkout-index` can apply `.gitattributes` transformations, while `git archive` can honor export rules. A pinned tree may contain hostile—or simply surprising—attributes, filters, encodings, Git LFS pointers, symlinks, or gitlinks. A byte-preserving materializer should neither run repository-specified helpers nor quietly hydrate external content. It must check that paths and modes can actually be represented on the target filesystem before granting access. Default macOS APFS may treat names differing only by case or Unicode normalization as the same path even though a Git tree distinguishes them. Such collisions require a failed setup or an explicitly different, authorized representation—not a silent overwrite. Git trees also omit empty directories, timestamps, ownership, extended attributes, and much other filesystem metadata.

**C. The importer is a security boundary.** The auditor's directory contains untrusted pathnames and filesystem objects even if the auditor is acting in good faith. A privileged scanner that checks a symlink with `lstat` and later opens the pathname can be redirected between the two operations. Use a least-privileged scanner restricted to the workspace, descriptor-relative traversal (`openat`-style operations), no-follow rules for every component, `fstat` after opening, and an explicit policy for mounts, hardlinks, symlinks, and special files. A conservative first version can reject cross-device traversal, multiply linked regular files, sockets, FIFOs, and devices, recording why. Whole-session quiescence is the primary defense against concurrent changes; careful traversal remains necessary defense in depth. The privileged custody writer should receive validated bytes and a manifest, not permission to browse arbitrary host paths.

**D. Git's staging conveniences can silently omit evidence.** `git stash create` normally omits untracked files; it also represents staged versus unstaged state that a plain-directory auditor need not have. `git add -A` can honor auditor-controlled `.gitignore` and `.gitattributes`; `git add -f` does not resolve every nested-repository or transformation problem. `git update-index --index-info` can ignore paths containing a `.git` component while returning success. A nested repository may otherwise be represented as a gitlink whose objects Stewart does not possess. The scanner's independent manifest must therefore be compared with the built tree, entry by entry. Unsupported names or objects must lead to a declared exception, authorized sidecar, or failed capture—not a successful-looking subset mislabeled complete. Care is also needed with Git object formats, shell/environment configuration, hooks, and external helpers.

**E. “No Git” and “no access to custody Git” are different promises.** Stewart can withhold `.git`, its inspection interface, credentials, and the Git executable supplied in a managed environment. It cannot truthfully promise that arbitrary code cannot implement Git-like algorithms or create unrelated repositories unless the execution environment imposes much stronger restrictions. The enforceable `--files-only` boundary should be precise: *Stewart supplies no Git facilities to the auditor and exposes none of its source or custody stores.* If the product promises a literal ban on invoking Git, that must be separately tested as an executor/sandbox policy, not inferred from the absence of `.git`. This must not turn Stewart into a general-purpose agent-behavior police force.

**F. Git reachability is not automatically durable custody.** Audit refs placed in the subject repository may leak through mirror operations and alter the repository Stewart was meant to investigate. A separate custody repo avoids that coupling, but an alternates pointer to subject objects can fail after the subject prunes them. Any durable retention claim must include the necessary object closure or another demonstrated pinning guarantee. A crash between writing blobs, creating a tree, updating a ref, and committing a receipt can leave partial state. Stewart needs verified readback, reconciliation after interruptions, and records that distinguish successful, partial, failed, or unverified publication. Git object IDs prove identity of the objects actually stored; they do not certify the completeness or intellectual truth of the audit.

**G. The architecture can fail by expansion even if the Git machinery works.** Workspace setup must not become choosing the auditor's strategy. Controlled inspection must not become unrestricted history access or autonomous evidence hunting. Custody capture must not become report adjudication, automatic repair, or a manager's decision on closure. Every material extension should be tested against the admission rule in Section 1. Equally, the desire for a perfectly tidy three-part diagram must not erase the commission, return types, authorization changes, interruptions, refusals, or manager disposition. They are the substance of custody, even though they are not additional operational responsibilities.

**Implementation threshold.** Before declaring the architecture or `--files-only` ready, demonstrate: a complete materialization on supported filesystems without checkout transformations; genuine separation of auditor and custody authority; session-end quiescence even with background descendants; traversal that cannot escape the workspace; a capture manifest that matches every retained tree entry and explicitly accounts for omissions; recoverable refs, objects, and receipts after simulated crashes; and a record that keeps `answer`/`needs-evidence`/`reframe`, work completion, capture status, and managerial disposition separate. An inventory of Stewart's current privileged operations must also show that each belongs to one of the three responsibilities or is moved outside the custodial core.

**Recommended provisional decision:** Accept the exclusive three-responsibility boundary as Stewart's architectural test. Accept `--files-only` as an optional restricted profile, initially with exit-only capture. Treat the Git plumbing above as promising implementation choices, not as mandatory doctrine: Stewart may use other tools wherever they provide a simpler, safer way to honor the same narrow promises.
